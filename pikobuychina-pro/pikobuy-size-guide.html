PikoBuy Size Guide: Measure Before You Order

Sizing is a specification problem. Define the fit you need, compare measurements and keep the chosen variant clear before purchase.

Reading time: 8 minutes · Apparel and footwear measurement guide.

Why letter sizes are not enough

A familiar size label can mean very different measurements across sellers, product cuts and markets. A “medium” in one listing may be much shorter, narrower or looser than a “medium” you own. The same applies to footwear: a number may refer to a different regional system, a different last or a different internal length. The safe approach is to treat a size label as a starting point, not a fit guarantee.

Before ordering, choose a comparable item you already own and like. Measure it flat and record the dimensions that affect the fit: chest width, shoulder width, garment length, sleeve length, waist, rise, inseam and leg opening as relevant. For footwear, record the insole length or foot length and note how much room you prefer. These references are more useful than an assumed conversion.

Read the live chart with the selected variant

Seller charts can be specific to a model, season or variant. Check that the chart belongs to the exact item and not a related product image. Note whether a measurement refers to the garment itself, a body recommendation or a range. Check the unit and compare like with like; a chest circumference is not the same as a flat pit-to-pit width.

Allow for ordinary measuring tolerance and construction differences, but do not use tolerance as a reason to ignore a large mismatch. If a chart is incomplete or unclear, treat that as an unresolved issue. A good purchasing request states the desired variant and size clearly and includes the measurement that is most important to your decision.

Fit questions for common clothing types

For T-shirts and hoodies, chest width, shoulder width, length and sleeve length usually explain fit more clearly than a label. Consider whether you want a fitted, regular or oversized silhouette and compare the listed cut with your reference garment. Fabric weight, shrinkage risk and ribbing can also change how the same measurements feel in wear.

For trousers, record waist, rise, inseam, thigh and leg opening. A waist label alone says little about a high-rise versus low-rise cut or a tapered versus straight leg. For jackets, check chest, shoulder, sleeve and total length, then consider the layers you expect to wear underneath. A technical outer layer and a padded winter jacket can require very different allowances.

Footwear needs a separate process

Start with the source size system and an internal-length measurement if available. Compare it with your foot length and the fit you prefer, while leaving sensible room for socks and movement. A seller’s conversion table can be helpful, but it should not override a directly stated insole or internal measurement without a reason.

When warehouse photos are available, a close-up of the size label and an insole measurement can help confirm that the selected variant appears consistent. The image still cannot guarantee comfort, width or long-term fit. Use it to verify the visible selection, then decide based on your measurement plan.

Keep the selected size visible through the workflow

Size mistakes often happen because the buyer checks a chart carefully, then loses track of the exact selected variant. Save the listing screenshot and add a one-line order note: item, colour, selected size, key measurement and preferred fit. Confirm that note before the first payment, and compare it with any visible size label at warehouse intake.

If an essential measurement is uncertain, ask before committing rather than hoping a later photo will solve it. A short focused question can prevent a costly decision: “Please confirm the flat chest width for size L” is far more useful than “Which size should I choose?”

Size ruleMeasure an item that fits you well, compare the same dimensions on the live chart, and keep the selected variant in your order note.

Make uncertainty explicit

There is no perfect universal conversion from one seller’s chart to another person’s fit preference. The responsible goal is not certainty where none exists; it is to reduce avoidable uncertainty with comparable measurements, a clear selected variant and an evidence-based final check.
