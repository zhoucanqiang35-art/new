Litbuy QC Photos: A Checklist Before Shipping

Save the seller listing, selected size and colour before ordering. When warehouse photos become available, compare the item, label, measurements and visible condition with that record. For shoes, inspect both sides, soles and size labels; for clothing compare chest and length measurements, seams and print placement; for bags check dimensions, straps and hardware.

Ask for a clearer angle or measurement when a detail is missing, and check the applicable return or exchange terms while the item remains in the warehouse. Images show only visible details: lighting can alter colour, and a photograph cannot establish authenticity, durability or fit.

Start with a written checklist. Record the listing URL or item ID, the exact option and any measurements that matter before the order changes status. When photos arrive, compare one point at a time instead of judging the item from a single overall image. Keep the original reference and the inspection date with the photos.

If measurements are shown, note the unit and how the item was laid out. Compare like with like: a flat garment measurement is not the same as a body measurement. A ruler hidden by a fold or a picture taken at an angle may not answer the question; request clarification rather than assuming precision.

A difference does not automatically mean a defect. Lighting, perspective and seller tolerances can affect appearance. Record the specific concern and ask the service about available options before approving shipment. Return eligibility depends on the seller, order and current policy; do not assume an exchange will be accepted.

Before requesting extra photographs, identify the missing evidence. A close-up of a seam answers a different question from a full-length image, and a ruler across the chest only helps if the garment is laid flat. Ask one specific question per image and retain the response with the order record.

For a shoe, compare both shoes together for visible symmetry and check the outsole, heel, toe and size labels. For a jacket, examine the fastening, pockets, lining and measurements. For a bag, inspect the handle attachments and hardware. A photo cannot test water resistance, load strength or durability; treat those as unknown.

For a measurement request, specify the dimension, unit and method: for example, the distance across a closed garment laid flat. Save the stated listing measurement next to the warehouse measurement. If the seller gives a tolerance, compare it too; if the method is unclear, ask before making a decision.

When the pictures appear inconsistent, name the exact area and request an additional view in neutral light. Keep the order ID and photo date. Ask what remedies are available before the parcel is submitted, and check the current seller and service conditions rather than promising a particular refund.

Use a decision threshold before inspecting: list the measurements and visible details that must match, and distinguish a cosmetic preference from a problem that would prevent use. For example, if a sleeve length matters, ask for a photo showing the tape from the shoulder seam to the cuff, with the garment flat. Compare that method with the seller’s chart. If the picture is cropped or the unit is unreadable, it is insufficient evidence, not proof that the item is wrong.

Create a reference before purchase. A useful reference contains the product identifier, seller name, selected colour and size, the size chart, and a screenshot of the description as it appeared when you ordered. Write down the measurement method too: for example, chest width measured flat from seam to seam. Listings and available options can change. If a later photograph appears different, the dated reference lets you ask a precise question about the item you actually ordered instead of relying on memory. Keep the reference private if it contains account or address information. It is a comparison aid, not evidence that the seller has guaranteed every pictured detail.

Start the inspection with identity and completeness. Match the order number to the warehouse record and confirm that the visible product type, colour, size label and quantity correspond to the selected option. Look for pairs where relevant: a left and a right shoe, or all components listed for an accessory. If the image does not show a label or an included part, mark that point as unknown and ask whether another view is available. A missing item in a photograph may be outside the frame; do not assume that it is absent until the warehouse clarifies. Resolve any mismatch before combining items into a parcel.

Inspect condition systematically. Look at the whole item first, then inspect closures, attachment points, visible stitching and surfaces. For shoes, compare the two toes and heels, then inspect the outsole and size labels. For clothing, look at the front and back, seams, fasteners and any visible marks. For a bag, look at strap attachments, zips and hardware. Photograph angle, compression and lighting can create apparent asymmetry or colour differences, so request a second view if the concern matters. These steps identify visible questions; they cannot establish whether an item is authentic or how it will perform after extended use.

Use measurements in a way that can be repeated. A size label alone does not predict fit across sellers. Compare a garment you already own using the same dimension and method as the listing: for example, lay both garments flat and compare width across the chest, rather than comparing a flat width to body circumference. Ask for a straight, readable tape and specify the start and end points. A measurement taken over a fold or at an angle may be misleading. If a difference is small, consider the seller’s stated tolerance, the fabric and measuring method. A photograph is useful evidence, but it is not a precision instrument.

Write a focused clarification request. Include the order reference, the precise area, the view or measurement needed, and the reason it matters. For example: please photograph the inside size label clearly and measure the insole from heel to toe in centimetres. Ask whether any additional photo service is available and whether a charge applies before requesting it. Give the service time to answer and save the response with the order. If the new view still does not answer the question, state what remains unclear. Do not share payment details or unrelated personal information in an inspection request.

Decide before parcel submission. Classify each item as accepted, awaiting clarification, or needing a discussion about available remedies. A return or exchange may depend on the seller’s conditions, deadlines and the stage of the order; confirm the live terms and any costs with the service. Record the decision and date, then check the warehouse deadline for that specific item. When an issue is accepted, note its limits so you can make an informed choice later. After you submit a parcel, your available options may differ. Keep the order ID, relevant photos, messages and parcel record together for any future support request.

Use the final review as a record of uncertainty as well as defects. An item can look acceptable from the available angles while its fabric composition, hidden seams or long-term performance remain unverified. Write down which features you inspected and which you could not see, then decide whether the remaining uncertainty is acceptable for your purchase. If a seller chart, order option and warehouse label use different sizing systems, ask for the actual measurements before interpreting the labels. Keep copies of your questions and the replies together with the dated order record. If you request a return, ask what deadlines, conditions, domestic shipping costs and service charges apply to that specific order. Only the service and seller can confirm the available remedy. This makes the checklist a decision tool rather than a promise that every problem can be detected from photographs.
