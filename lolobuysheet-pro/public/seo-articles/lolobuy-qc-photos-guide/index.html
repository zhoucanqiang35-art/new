LoloBuy QC Photos Guide: What Warehouse Inspection Can and Cannot Prove

A detailed guide to LoloBuy warehouse QC photos, the official standard-inspection scope, common blind spots and the evidence to request before shipping.

Reviewed August 17, 202612 min readEnglish long-form guide
Fact-checking note

Warehouse photos are one of the most useful parts of an agent order, but they are also easy to overrate. A clear photograph can prove that a visible detail existed at a particular moment. It cannot prove everything about the product. The practical goal of QC is therefore not to ask whether an item is “perfect.” It is to collect enough evidence to decide whether the order matches what you selected and whether any visible problem should be resolved before international shipping.

This guide is based on LoloBuy's published standard-inspection scope and related warehouse information checked on August 17, 2026. It explains the official service first, then turns that limited evidence into a repeatable review method. Product listings, seller policies and value-added services change, so the options shown in your warehouse account remain the final reference.

What LoloBuy's standard inspection actually covers

LoloBuy describes standard quality inspection as a free warehouse service. Its published scope includes visible checks of quantity, style, colour, size, model and obvious damage or defects. The standard service normally provides three to six photos. That is a meaningful baseline: you can compare what arrived with the order record before the item leaves China.

The wording “visible” matters. Warehouse staff can photograph the item, read accessible labels and notice obvious surface damage. Standard inspection does not automatically include every measurement, every internal component or a professional assessment of authenticity. A sealed product may remain sealed. Specialist electronics are not function-tested or authenticated simply because a photo has been uploaded.

Think of the standard set as a first-pass exception check. It should catch a red jacket ordered as black, one shoe when a pair was expected, a visibly crushed box, an incorrect model label or a large stain. It may not reveal a small stitching issue on the reverse side, the exact insole length, the feel of a fabric, a battery problem or a defect hidden by packaging.

Start by checking the order record, not the product's appearance

The fastest way to make a bad QC decision is to begin with “Do I like how it looks?” Start with the order facts. Open the original listing and the LoloBuy order details beside the warehouse images. Confirm seller, product, selected variant, colour, size, quantity and any note you gave the shopping agent.

This comparison separates an order mismatch from a quality preference. If the seller sent the wrong colour, the evidence is the selected colour in the order record and the visible colour in the warehouse photo. If the item is the ordered colour but you no longer like it, that may fall under different seller return terms. Precise evidence makes the agent's conversation with the seller easier.

Also check whether the listing itself changed after purchase. Sellers sometimes update images, options or product descriptions. Save the version of the listing and selection visible when you ordered. A current page that no longer shows the original option can otherwise make a later dispute harder to explain.

Review the image set in a fixed order

I use the same order every time because it prevents attention from jumping to one attractive detail while missing a basic mismatch.

  1. Count: Is the correct number of pieces present? Are paired or bundled items complete?
  2. Identity: Does the visible model, colour, size or option match the order?
  3. Overall condition: Look for crushing, stains, tears, scratches, major asymmetry or obvious manufacturing damage.
  4. Critical details: Check the areas that affect use, such as soles, zips, closures, ports, straps, print alignment or hardware.
  5. Packaging and labels: Decide whether the box, tags or protective materials matter for your use or a possible return.
  6. Blind spots: List what is not visible and decide whether any missing evidence changes your willingness to accept the item.

Zoom in on the original image files when possible. A small gallery thumbnail can hide stains, scratches and label text. Do not judge colour too precisely from one photograph because lighting, white balance and screen settings can shift it. Use colour evidence mainly to identify a clear mismatch, not a subtle shade difference.

QC for shoes: measure the decision, not the hype

For footwear, begin with the ordered size label and whether both shoes form the correct pair. Then compare the overall shape, sole attachment, visible glue, stitching, toe area, heel alignment, logos and obvious damage. If fit matters, a label alone is not enough. Request an insole or outsole measurement through the appropriate service if that option is available.

A photograph cannot tell you how a shoe will feel after an hour of walking. It also cannot guarantee materials or long-term sole durability. If the seller provides a size chart, compare its measurement method with the measurement you request. “Size 42” is less useful than a clearly photographed measurement taken in the same way as the chart.

Shoe boxes create a separate decision. Removing a box can reduce parcel volume, but LoloBuy's July 14, 2026 notice says products cannot be returned or exchanged after original packaging is removed. Finish the item decision first. If one box must be retained, follow the platform's parcel remark or support procedure rather than assuming the warehouse will know which one matters.

QC for clothing: labels are not fit tests

For clothing, verify the selected size, colour and quantity, then inspect the front, back, seams, print or embroidery, collar, cuffs, zips and visible marks. A standard photo may show the size tag, but it does not prove the garment's actual dimensions or fit.

If measurements matter, request specific points such as chest width, body length, shoulder width, waist or inseam. State how the measurement should be taken. Two people can measure the same garment differently if one stretches the fabric or follows a curved seam. A clear request is more useful than “please measure everything.”

Be realistic about colour and texture. Warehouse lighting can shift colour, and a photograph cannot reliably communicate thickness, softness or stretch. Use the images to catch an obvious colour or material mismatch, then rely on listing information and seller evidence for characteristics the warehouse cannot prove visually.

QC for bags and accessories: focus on function points

For a bag, belt or small accessory, check the selected size and colour, then inspect straps, handles, zips, clasps, holes, hardware, printed details, edges and visible scratches. Ask for the dimensions if the item's usefulness depends on fitting a phone, laptop or other object.

Small hardware problems can be hard to see from an overall image. If a clasp, screw, hinge or connector is the part most likely to fail, request a close-up. A still photo may show that the part is present, but it may not prove smooth operation. Do not claim a function test unless the warehouse service explicitly performed and recorded one.

QC for electronics and sealed products has stricter limits

Electronics are where users most often expect more than a standard visual inspection can deliver. A warehouse photo can show the model printed on accessible packaging, the plug type, colour, visible accessories and exterior damage. It does not prove battery capacity, internal components, software status, network compatibility or long-term operation.

If the product is sealed, opening it may affect the seller's return conditions or the product's value. LoloBuy's standard-inspection limits should be treated conservatively: do not interpret an unopened box photo as confirmation that the contents are correct or functional. Use only value-added checks that the platform actually offers and that you understand before approving the item.

When to request extra photos or measurements

An extra request is justified when the missing evidence could change the accept-or-return decision. Examples include an unreadable size label, a suspected stain, a connector type, a shoe measurement, a garment measurement, a serial or model detail, the underside of an accessory or damage on packaging that may have reached the product.

Write the request like a camera instruction. “Please photograph the white size label inside the left shoe so all text is readable” is better than “more pictures please.” “Measure the chest from armpit to armpit with the garment flat and without stretching” is better than “check size.” Precise instructions reduce misunderstanding and make the result easier to compare.

Do not order extra images merely to create a larger gallery. More photographs are useful only when they close a decision-relevant gap. The question is always what uncertainty the next image is supposed to resolve.

How to handle a visible problem

Save the relevant order detail and warehouse image, describe the problem in neutral terms, and identify the requested remedy. For example: “The order shows black, but the warehouse image shows blue. Please ask the seller to exchange it for the selected black option.” Avoid broad claims that the photos cannot prove.

Return and exchange eligibility depends on seller terms, timing, product condition and domestic freight. LoloBuy can communicate and handle the domestic process, but the warehouse photo does not create an unlimited return right. Act promptly while the item remains separate and before any irreversible service.

Packaging removal changes the after-sales position

Packaging removal can reduce international shipping weight or volume, especially for large shoe boxes or unnecessary outer packaging. It should happen after QC, not before. LoloBuy's published July 14 notice is explicit that an item cannot be returned or exchanged after its original packaging has been removed.

That rule turns the sequence into a simple discipline: inspect, request extra evidence, resolve seller issues, accept the item, and only then choose package removal. If you reverse those steps, a small shipping saving can remove the practical remedy for a much larger product problem.

A QC record worth keeping

  • Original product URL and seller name
  • Screenshot of the selected variant and order notes
  • All standard warehouse images
  • Any paid close-ups or measurements
  • Dates of warehouse arrival and messages
  • Return or exchange request and seller response
  • Final acceptance decision
  • Packaging services selected
  • Parcel number containing the item

This record is useful even when nothing goes wrong. It lets you compare the product received with the warehouse evidence and improve future requests. It also prevents a spreadsheet, seller listing, warehouse photo and final parcel from becoming four disconnected pieces of information.

Good QC is not about pretending a photograph can eliminate risk. It is about using the official inspection scope honestly, identifying what the photos can prove, and asking for targeted evidence where they cannot. If the evidence is not strong enough for the decision, pause before shipping. Once the item is packed internationally, the cheaper and simpler seller-return stage may already be behind you.
