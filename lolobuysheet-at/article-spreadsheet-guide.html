How to use a product spreadsheet before you search

A practical way to turn a broad fashion idea into a checkable shortlist — without treating a spreadsheet as a recommendation or a promise.

Independent research · Reviewed 8 September 2026

Factual scope. This guide uses only the current LoloBuy homepage statement reviewed for this edition: warehouse arrivals are inspected for defects, size, colour, and other details. It does not claim country coverage, prices, delivery times, seller availability, or guarantees.

Start with a decision, not a scroll

A spreadsheet looks simple because the first thing you see is a list. The useful work happens before the list: decide what question you are trying to answer. “I want something good” cannot be tested. “I need a dark, relaxed cotton hoodie with a specific fit and a budget I can accept” can. That difference matters because a search result is not proof that an item suits you; it is only a place to begin looking.

Write one sentence that describes the decision. Include the category, the use case, and the one or two features that would make you reject an item. A shopper looking for a daily layer may care about fit and fabric first. A shopper looking for footwear may care about size reference, construction details, and the exact colourway. The goal is not to make a complicated brief. It is to stop the category page from becoming an endless feed of attractive but incomparable links.

Treat the spreadsheet as a map, not a verdict. It can help you discover terms, group related items, and narrow a broad field. It cannot tell you, on its own, whether a current listing is available, whether a variant is still selectable, or whether the final product will match your expectation. Those are product-level questions. Keeping that boundary clear is the first defence against impulsive research.

A useful starting note has three lines: what you are looking for, what evidence you need, and what would make you walk away. For example: “black overshirt; need a visible size reference and clear option naming; walk away if the record lacks a specific variant.” This small note makes the next ten minutes more valuable than an hour of unstructured browsing.

Before searching, define your budget, intended use and non-negotiable features. This creates a short list of comparable options.

Build a record you can revisit

When you open a candidate, capture the facts that would let another person find the same item later. Start with the source link, product name, category, selected variant, and the date you looked. Add a short note in your own words about why it made the shortlist. Do not copy a description just because it sounds confident. If the description does not answer your question, record that uncertainty instead.

The distinction between a product name and a product record is important. Names are often vague, repeated, translated differently, or reused across variants. A record gives the name context: which option was selected, what the size or colour field said, what material or measurement information was visible, and which image or detail made it relevant. That context is what allows you to compare candidates honestly later.

Use one convention for unknown information. A blank cell can look like an accidental omission; “not shown at review” is clear. The same is true for a claim you cannot verify. Mark it as a seller statement, a listing statement, or an unconfirmed note rather than converting it into a fact. This habit feels slower while you are doing it, but it is much faster than returning to a dozen open tabs to remember why each one was saved.

Keep your record narrow. You do not need a biography of every item. In most cases, a source link, exact option, visible sizing information, visible colour wording, a few comparison notes, and a review date are enough. The record should make a later decision easier, not create a second research project.

Save the original product address, item ID, selected variant and date so your choice remains clear if a link changes.

Search with terms that can be checked

Good search phrases contain features you can recognize in a listing. Begin with the category, then add one material, silhouette, construction, or colour term. “Zip hoodie heavyweight” is more useful than “best hoodie”; “low-profile leather sneaker” is more useful than “cool shoes.” The first phrase gives you things to inspect. The second asks a page to make a judgment it cannot reliably make for you.

Use synonyms deliberately, not randomly. A relaxed shirt can be described as boxy, oversized, straight, workwear, or utility depending on the listing. Search one term at a time and note which one consistently produces relevant records. If a term brings unrelated results, remove it. This is closer to editing a question than to playing a keyword lottery.

Categories are a guardrail. Start in the closest category, then broaden only when the evidence says you should. If a specific product appears under accessories but your decision is about apparel fit, it may not belong on the same comparison sheet. Keeping categories distinct prevents a list from mixing items that require different proof.

Once you have a shortlist, run a second search using the exact terms that appeared in the strongest records. This is a human advantage of a spreadsheet: one good listing can teach you the vocabulary to find related items. The aim is not to replicate any seller wording; it is to develop a clearer way of describing what you actually want.

Compare the same details for each result: price, available sizes, stated material and visible information. Recheck current details before ordering.

Compare evidence instead of impressions

A useful comparison asks the same questions of every candidate. Can you identify the selected option? Is there a size reference or measurement information? Is the colour named clearly enough to distinguish it from nearby variants? Is the material or construction described, shown, or simply unknown? When each record receives the same questions, you can see where the evidence is strong and where you are filling gaps with hope.

Separate “I like it” from “I can verify it.” Both are valid notes, but they do different jobs. A strong visual impression may earn an item a place on your watchlist. It should not overwrite missing option detail, unclear sizing, or a record that you cannot locate again. This is especially useful when several candidates look similar in product photos but differ in the information that supports a purchase decision.

Do not turn a photo into a technical claim. A photo can show a visible feature; it cannot reliably prove fabric weight, durability, authenticity, or a precise shade under every light. Phrase your note around what is actually visible: “contrast stitching appears visible in the supplied image” is more honest than “high-quality stitching.” Careful language protects the research from becoming marketing copy.

When two items remain close, compare the risk of ambiguity, not only the appeal. The better candidate may be the one with a clearer option name, a more usable sizing reference, or a record you can find again. That is not a glamorous conclusion, but it is the kind that reduces avoidable mistakes.

Measure a garment that fits and compare chest width, length and sleeves with the specific listing’s size chart. Different measurement methods can produce variation.

Use platform information at its stated scope

One current, checkable statement from LoloBuy’s homepage is that, when products reach its warehouse, the team inspects items for defects and checks size, colour, and other details. That is a useful checkpoint to understand, but it should be kept at the scope the platform states. It does not automatically answer every question a shopper may have about a particular listing or final outcome.

Before you rely on any inspection step, write down what matters for your item. For a garment, that may be the selected size, visible colour, obvious defects, and a specific construction point. For footwear, it may be the pair, size marking, colourway, and obvious condition. A specific checklist gives you something meaningful to compare with the information that is actually available.

Do not enlarge a general inspection statement into a guarantee about authenticity, material composition, exact fit, future availability, delivery, or return outcomes. If one of those questions matters, look for the platform’s current terms or ask through its official support route before payment. An independent guide should make these limits visible rather than bury them under reassuring language.

The practical lesson is simple: inspection information works best as one stage in a documented process. It complements careful selection and a clear record; it does not replace them. The better your pre-search notes are, the easier it is to recognize a meaningful mismatch later.

A catalog image is not a warehouse photo of your item or proof of authenticity or durability. Note missing views and resolve essential questions before paying.

Know when the research is complete

Research is complete when you can explain your choice in plain language. You should be able to say which candidate you selected, which exact option you meant, what evidence supported it, and what uncertainty remains. If you cannot do that without reopening a dozen tabs, the record is not ready yet.

Use a stop rule. You might decide to select the first candidate that meets every essential requirement, or to compare only three records before choosing. Without a stop rule, a spreadsheet can turn into an excuse to keep looking for a perfect answer that no listing can provide. The right time to stop is when further searching is not changing the decision criteria.

Before moving to a product search, check the source link, selected option, size or measurement reference, visible colour wording, and your review date one final time. Then preserve a short note about the uncertainty you accepted. That note is not a failure; it is evidence that you made a conscious choice rather than forgetting what was missing.

The strongest spreadsheet habit is not finding the most links. It is leaving a clear trail from question to evidence to next action. Use the database only after that trail exists, and keep the final purchase decision tied to the current information you can see for yourself.

Record why each candidate made the shortlist and what remains unknown. Open the matching category or product and reconfirm price, availability and variant.
